ACM CCS 2026#

A security conference is not this PM's home turf, but CCS 2026's program intersects their stack heavily: eBPF, programmable data planes, DPU offload, BGP/RPKI/IXP interconnect risk, GPU interconnect and multi-tenant serving, and LLM-agent tooling. One caveat applies to everything below: none of these items has a published abstract or paper link in the program — each entry lists title, authors, and session only. Every write-up is therefore grounded in the title and author affiliations, with mechanisms flagged as inferred where they are. Treat this as a watch-list of what to read when the papers appear, not a verdict on results.

November 15, 2026 – November 19, 2026 Program

Core#

NVBleed: Covert and Side-Channel Attacks on NVIDIA Multi-GPU Interconnect#

Yicheng Zhang, Ravan Nazaraliyev (UC Riverside), Sankha Baran Dutta (Brookhaven National Laboratory), Andres Marquez, Kevin J. Barker (Pacific Northwest National Laboratory), Nael Abu-Ghazaleh (UC Riverside)

  • Why: GPU interconnects (NVLink/NVSwitch-class fabrics) are the shared substrate of every AI training cluster, and increasingly of multi-tenant inference. Cross-GPU covert and side channels change the threat model for exactly the hardware this PM builds on — especially when GPUs from different tenants or jobs share a fabric.
  • What: Covert and side-channel attacks against NVIDIA's multi-GPU interconnect. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: The "bleed" in the name suggests information leaking across GPUs that share an interconnect, likely via timing or contention channels on the link fabric; the actual channel construction is not published yet. The PNNL/Brookhaven co-authorship hints at relevance to HPC-scale GPU systems.
  • Where applicable: NVIDIA-specific by definition, but the technique class applies to any NVLink-based cluster, GPU virtualization, or multi-job GPU sharing. Directly relevant if you run shared GPU fabrics for training or inference.

LDBF: Deep Exploration of eBPF Runtime via LLM-Driven Program Generation and Adaptive Context Orchestration#

Yinhao Hu, Kaiyan Mei, Quan Sun, Dongliang Mu (Huazhong University of Science and Technology), Zeyu Sun (Chinese Academy of Sciences), Yuan Li (Zhongguancun Laboratory), Chao Zhang (Tsinghua University)

  • Why: Two of the PM's interests in one paper: eBPF (the substrate under Cilium and most modern K8s networking) and LLM-assisted systems engineering. Using LLMs to systematically generate eBPF programs to probe the runtime is both a hardening effort and a preview of how infrastructure testing will be done.
  • What: A system that uses LLM-driven program generation, with adaptive orchestration of context, to deeply explore the eBPF runtime. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred from the title: an LLM generates large volumes of diverse eBPF programs, with feedback ("adaptive context") steering generation toward unexplored or buggy corners of the verifier/JIT/runtime. Details pending the paper.
  • Where applicable: The eBPF runtime is Linux-kernel-wide, so findings apply broadly wherever eBPF runs — K8s nodes, Cilium deployments, observability agents. The LLM-driven methodology itself transfers to other interpreter/VM hardening problems.

TurboRetry: Mitigating Large-Scale QUIC Handshake Floods with Off-the-Shelf DPU Offloading#

Jiahao Wu, Kai Lv, Zhenyu Li (Institute of Computing Technology, CAS), Heng Pan, Yanbiao Li, Gaogang Xie (Computer Network Information Center, CAS)

  • Why: QUIC handshake floods are a live operational DoS vector at any HTTP/3 edge, and offloading the mitigation to commodity DPUs speaks directly to the PM's DPU/SmartNIC and front-end infrastructure interests. "Off-the-shelf" matters: no custom silicon assumed.
  • What: A mitigation for large-scale QUIC handshake floods built on DPU offloading. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: The name points at QUIC's stateless retry/token mechanism; the likely design moves retry-token handling and handshake filtering onto the DPU's datapath so the host CPU never sees flood traffic. Flagged as inferred from the title.
  • Where applicable: Any QUIC/HTTP-3 serving edge with DPU-equipped hosts — cloud front-ends, CDNs, API gateways. The off-the-shelf framing suggests broad applicability rather than a single-vendor silicon dependency.

Parade: Unified and Data-Driven Prefix-AS Semantics for BGP Anomaly Detection#

Yunze Wei, Xingang Shi, Minghao Zhang, Zhaozhen Wang, Han Zhang, Yahui Li, Xia Yin (Tsinghua University), Qiang Ma (Zhongguancun Laboratory)

  • Why: BGP anomaly detection (hijacks, route leaks) is a daily operational concern for anyone operating or interconnecting networks. A unified prefix-AS semantic model is the kind of thing that could improve monitoring tools beyond signature-based approaches.
  • What: A data-driven BGP anomaly detection framework built on unified prefix-AS semantics. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred from the title: modeling the joint semantics of prefix-AS relationships (who legitimately originates what) as the basis for detecting anomalies, rather than per-signal heuristics. "Data-driven" suggests learned models over routing data. The Tsinghua networking group typically validates on real BGP measurement data — unverified until the paper.
  • Where applicable: Broadly applicable to BGP monitoring: route collectors, ISP monitoring, interconnect security. Not tied to a single operator's topology by design.

Exploiting vulnerabilities at IXP route servers to perform stealth BGP hijacks#

Gabby Rimlinger (Sorbonne Université), Joaquim Pereira (University of Waikato), Matthieu Gouel (nxthdr), Olivier Fourmaux, Timur Friedman (Sorbonne Université), Pedro Marcos (FURG), Cristel Pelsser (UCLouvain), Ronaldo A. Ferreira (UFMS), Kevin Vermeulen (CNRS, École Polytechnique)

  • Why: IXPs are where the PM's interconnect strategy plays out, and route servers are the mechanism most mid-size networks use to exchange routes without a full mesh of BGP sessions. If route servers can be abused for stealthy hijacks, that changes how much route-server-learned routes can be trusted.
  • What: Demonstrated vulnerabilities in IXP route servers enabling BGP hijacks that evade common detection. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: manipulation of route-server path handling or visibility rules (e.g., what is filtered, re-advertised, or hidden from route-views) so a hijacked prefix doesn't look hijacked to standard monitors. The author team (Sorbonne/LIP6, CNRS, UCLouvain — established IXP measurement researchers) suggests real route-server and IXP data involvement, pending the paper.
  • Where applicable: Any network that peers via IXP route servers — which is most of them. Directly actionable for interconnect/peering policy review.

Enforcement of In-Kernel Stateful Security Policies via eBPF#

Letterio Galletta (IMT School for Advanced Studies Lucca)

  • Why: eBPF is maturing from observability into enforcement — exactly the trajectory the PM tracks via Cilium and friends. Stateful in-kernel policy is the hard part (connection state, protocol-aware rules) and this sits in a session on formal analysis and verification, suggesting rigor rather than a hack.
  • What: A system for enforcing stateful security policies directly in the Linux kernel using eBPF. (No public abstract — title and session only; single-author talk.)
  • How: Inferred from the title: flow/connection state tracked in eBPF maps so policy decisions happen in-kernel without a userspace agent or sidecar round-trip. Placement in the formal-analysis session suggests the policy model or its enforcement has a verification component.
  • Where applicable: Linux hosts and K8s nodes broadly — no special silicon or operator topology implied. Relevant to anyone replacing sidecar-based policy enforcement with in-kernel enforcement.

Diagnosing Programmable Data Plane Attacks with Provenance Observability#

Dhiraj Saharia (Georgetown University), Ashish Gehani, Vinod Yegneswaran (SRI International), Benjamin E. Ujcich (Georgetown University)

  • Why: P4/programmable dataplanes are a core interest, and this paper pairs two things the field needs: an attack surface analysis for programmable planes, and provenance-based observability as the diagnosis tool — operational tooling thinking applied to a research fabric.
  • What: Techniques for diagnosing attacks on programmable data planes using provenance observability. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: capturing lineage of packets and events through the programmable pipeline so that when an attack occurs, its causal path through the data plane can be reconstructed — a forensic step up from flow counters.
  • Where applicable: P4-programmable switches and SmartNIC-based pipelines; relevant wherever programmable dataplanes are deployed or evaluated. The Georgetown/SRI pedigree suggests a research-to-production tooling angle.

eMicro: Real-Time Multi-Hop Access Control for Microservices with eBPF#

Rizky Ramadhana Putra, Osama Bajaber (King Abdulaziz University), Saimon Amanuel Tsegai, Teryl Taylor, Frederico Araujo (IBM Research), Yuede Ji (UT Arlington), Peng Gao (Virginia Tech)

  • Why: Microsegmentation for microservices is the service-mesh/Cilium problem the PM works on daily. Multi-hop, real-time access control without sidecars is precisely the zero-trust architecture question for Kubernetes environments.
  • What: eBPF-based real-time access control across multi-hop microservice call chains. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred from the title: eBPF hooks enforcing access-control policy at each service hop in the call path, with "real-time" implying low enough overhead to enforce on the hot path rather than audit after the fact. IBM Research co-authorship suggests enterprise-microservice motivation.
  • Where applicable: Microservice/Kubernetes environments broadly — the eBPF substrate is generic. An alternative or complement to sidecar-based service meshes and network policies.

TWIST: Private Tenant-Dedicated LLM Serving via Joint Model-Token Transformation#

Qizhi Zhang, Yu Lin, Wenqiang Ruan, Quanwei Cai, Daode Zhang, Jue Hong, Ye Wu (ByteDance), Yufei Chen, Longxiang Wang, Cong Wang (City University of Hong Kong)

  • Why: Multi-tenant LLM serving with per-tenant isolation is a live problem for anyone offering inference as a service. If the title's approach delivers "tenant-dedicated" serving without a model copy per tenant, it changes the economics of isolated inference.
  • What: A technique for private, tenant-dedicated LLM serving via joint model-token transformation. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred from the title: jointly transforming the model and the token space per tenant so each tenant's serving instance appears dedicated and isolated — presumably cheaper isolation than per-tenant weights. Mechanism unverified until the paper.
  • Where applicable: LLM inference platforms, especially multi-tenant ones. ByteDance co-authorship suggests motivation from large-scale production serving — an org-specific scale caveat to check for in the evaluation.

Adjacent#

Rethinking Isolation in RPKI: Cross-Publication-Origin Attacks on RPKI Validation#

Qi Wang, Jianjun Chen, TaoFei Guo (Peking University), Minghao Zhang, Yuheng Zhang, Jingcheng Yang, Xia Yin (Tsinghua University)

  • Why: RPKI is the routing-integrity layer the PM's interconnect strategy leans on. A new attack class ("cross-publication-origin") against RPKI validation — not against key hygiene — would change operational trust in ROA-based route origin validation.
  • What: An attack class on RPKI validation arising from insufficient isolation between publication points. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred from the title: confusion or manipulation across publication origins such that data published for one origin influences validation of another — likely involving repository/publication-point structure rather than mis-issuance by CAs.
  • Where applicable: Every network operating RPKI (ROAs + validation). Not operator-specific; this is protocol/ecosystem structure, not topology.

The Trade-off Between Performance and Security: Exploring Vulnerabilities in DNS Task Queue Scheduling#

Shiming Liu, Yunyi Zhang, Ruixuan Li, Shiyao Guo, Baojun Liu, Donghong Sun (Tsinghua University), Yong Ma, Linjian Song (Alibaba Group)

  • Why: Resolver scheduling under crafted load is a real resiliency topic for DNS operations, and the performance-vs-security framing is the classic DNS-software tension. Alibaba co-authors suggest exposure of these issues at production scale.
  • What: Vulnerabilities arising from how DNS software schedules its task queue, explored as a performance/security trade-off. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: crafted query mixes that abuse queue/scheduling priorities to starve or degrade resolver processing — a DoS class that exists because scheduling optimizes for the benign traffic mix.
  • Where applicable: DNS resolver implementations broadly; the Alibaba Group involvement hints at production-resolver relevance (unverified). Matters to anyone running recursive/authoritative infrastructure.

LeakyTree: Cross-VM Bit-by-Bit Deduplicated Page Leakage via Linux Kernel Samepage Merging Internals#

Ernesto Martinez Garcia, Lukas Maar, Martin Unterguggenberger, Mathias Oberhuber, Stefan Mangard (Graz University of Technology)

  • Why: KSM memory deduplication is a standard memory-saving trick in virtualized/cloud hosts. Cross-VM leakage through it is a multi-tenancy risk for exactly the cloud infrastructure the PM runs.
  • What: Bit-by-bit cross-VM information leakage exploiting internals of Linux kernel samepage merging. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: side channels from KSM's dedup decisions (e.g., timing of merge/unmerge) letting a malicious VM infer another VM's memory contents one bit at a time. Graz has a strong side-channel track record, suggesting careful construction — pending the paper.
  • Where applicable: Any virtualization or cloud stack that enables KSM across trust domains. The mitigation question (disable cross-VM KSM) is directly operational.

Gone in 8 Seconds: Uncovering Malicious Bots Operating on Public Container Registries#

Billy Tsouvalas, Nick Nikiforakis (Stony Brook University)

  • Why: Public container registries are part of the cloud-native supply chain every K8s shop pulls from. Evidence of systematic malicious bot activity there informs image-provenance policy and registry trust.
  • What: A measurement study uncovering malicious bots operating on public container registries. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: large-scale measurement of automated account/behavior patterns on public registries (Stony Brook's measurement group is well established in this style of work).
  • Where applicable: Public registries and any organization pulling third-party images — supply-chain policy input rather than a specific defense.

ContractGuard: Auditing Semantic Contracts of MCP Tools for Security Violations#

Hengkai Ye, Zhechang Zhang, Ruibo Lu, Jinyuan Jia, Hong Hu (The Pennsylvania State University)

  • Why: MCP is becoming the de facto tool interface for LLM agents, and the PM's interests include agents. Auditing the semantic contracts of MCP tools is early plumbing for agent-to-tool trust — the layer where agent security will actually be enforced.
  • What: A system that audits the semantic contracts of MCP tools for security violations. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: static or semantic analysis of tool descriptions/schemas and their implementations to detect contracts that mislead agents or permit unsafe actions when invoked.
  • Where applicable: MCP-based agent deployments and tool ecosystems generally; relevant to anyone building or consuming agent tool catalogs.

From Prompt to Pwn: Exploiting GPU Memory Errors During ML Inference#

Jonas Roels, Adriaan Jacobs, Stijn Volckaert (KU Leuven), Silviu Vlasceanu, Mahmoud Ammar (Independent researchers)

  • Why: As inference fleets scale, "prompt reaches memory error" is a new integrity/availability class for AI infrastructure — the kind of failure mode a PM running GPU inference services needs on the risk register.
  • What: Exploitation of GPU memory errors during ML inference, reachable from model inputs. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: crafted prompts (shapes/sequences) that steer inference into execution paths where uncorrected memory errors can be turned into exploitable state — i.e., the model input becomes a fault-injection vector.
  • Where applicable: GPU inference services; the requirement of latent memory errors on the hardware is a real constraint on exploitability, but the class matters for fleet reliability even absent an attacker.

ReasoningBomb: A Stealthy Denial-of-Service Attack by Inducing Pathologically Long Reasoning in Large Reasoning Models#

Xiaogeng Liu (Johns Hopkins University), Xinyan Wang (University of Wisconsin–Madison), Yechao Zhang (Nanyang Technological University), Sanjay Kariyappa, Chong Xiang, G. Edward Suh (NVIDIA Corporation), Muhao Chen (UC Davis), Chaowei Xiao (Johns Hopkins University)

  • Why: This attacks inference economics directly: a request that looks innocuous but burns an extreme token budget is a capacity/cost attack on any reasoning-model endpoint. For anyone serving LLMs, this is the inference-era slowloris.
  • What: A stealthy DoS attack that induces pathologically long reasoning in large reasoning models. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: crafted inputs that trigger degenerate reasoning loops (excessive chain-of-thought length) while remaining inconspicuous to input-level filters — degrading serving capacity without detectable malicious content.
  • Where applicable: Any reasoning-model serving endpoint; NVIDIA co-authorship suggests attention to real inference-stack implications. Mitigations (token budgets, reasoning-length monitors) are operationally cheap to consider.

Mitigating Code Injection Attacks on Modern GPUs#

Carlo Ramponi, Luigi Dell'Eva (University of Trento), Adriaan Jacobs, Stijn Volckaert (KU Leuven), Silviu Vlasceanu, Mahmoud Ammar (Huawei Research), Bruno Crispo (University of Trento)

  • Why: The defensive counterpart to the GPU-attack papers above. As GPU compute becomes multi-tenant, code-injection defenses on GPU runtimes are part of the isolation story for shared accelerator fleets.
  • What: Mitigations against code injection attacks on modern GPUs. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: runtime enforcement on GPU binaries/executables (CFI-style checks or memory-protection changes) to block injection into running GPU code; mechanism unverified.
  • Where applicable: Modern GPU runtimes and any multi-tenant GPU compute. Huawei Research involvement may mean specific hardware scope — check the paper for which GPU generations are covered.

Governing AI Agent with Capability and Delegation Chain#

Jiaqin Yan, Guoxing Chen, Yan Meng, Haojin Zhu (Shanghai Jiao Tong University)

  • Why: Agent governance is emerging as the authorization model for autonomous agents — squarely in the PM's agents interest. Capability-plus-delegation chains is a familiar security primitive applied to a new subject.
  • What: A governance mechanism for AI agents based on capabilities and delegation chains. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: capability-based access control where agents hold explicitly granted capabilities, and delegation chains track how authority is passed along — so an agent cannot exceed what was granted, and sub-delegations remain auditable.
  • Where applicable: Agent frameworks generally; relevant to anyone designing what agents are allowed to do in production.

Trapped Handler: A Practical Performance Degradation Attack on NVIDIA vGPU via Preemption Manipulation#

Cheng Gu, Yanan Guo (University of Rochester), Fatemeh Moradihaghighi, Zhenkai Zhang (Clemson University), Daniel Genkin (Georgia Tech), Zhuo Li (Hydrox AI), Mengyuan Li (USC)

  • Why: Performance isolation in GPU virtualization is a core multi-tenancy concern for GPU clouds. A practical attack on the vGPU preemption path is direct input into vGPU scheduling configuration and noisy-neighbor risk assessment.
  • What: A practical performance degradation attack on NVIDIA vGPU exploiting preemption manipulation. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: abusing the preemption mechanism (forcing frequent or pathological preemption via the "handler") so co-tenants on the same physical GPU suffer degraded throughput — a noisy-neighbor attack rather than a data compromise.
  • Where applicable: NVIDIA vGPU deployments specifically (VDI, GPU clouds). Vendor-specific; the general lesson (preemption is an attack surface for performance isolation) transfers to other sliced-GPU schemes.

Where Have All the Firewalls Gone? Security Consequences of Residential IPv6 Transition#

Erik Rye (Johns Hopkins University), Dave Levin (University of Maryland), Robert Beverly (San Diego State University)

  • Why: IPv6 transition in residential space changes how much of the internet is directly exposed — with consequences for threat-modeling, scanning surface, and the assumption that NAT doubles as a firewall. Measurement pedigree here (Rye/Levin/Beverly) is strong.
  • What: A measurement study of the security consequences of residential IPv6 transition. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: comparing firewall prevalence and filtering behavior across IPv4 and IPv6 residential address space — likely internet-scale scanning/measurement to quantify how the transition changed exposure.
  • Where applicable: Residential ISPs and home networks; the findings inform anyone reasoning about the IPv6 threat surface, and are relevant to IPv6 deployment strategy generally.

A First-Principles Evaluation of Graph-Based Network Intrusion Detection Systems ( video )#

Rui Zhao, Wajih UI Hassan (University of Virginia)

  • Why: Graph-based NIDS with ML are heavily marketed; a first-principles evaluation asks whether the class actually delivers — the kind of skeptical, methodology-grounded result that should inform NID tooling decisions rather than vendor claims.
  • What: A first-principles evaluation of graph-based network intrusion detection systems. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only; a video recording exists.)
  • How: Inferred: systematic re-examination of the assumptions, feature claims, and evaluation methodology of graph NIDS — likely including controlled comparisons that isolate what the graph structure contributes.
  • Where applicable: NID deployments and SOC tooling choices broadly; findings should be methodology- rather than topology-specific, which is exactly why a first-principles treatment is valuable.

Wildcard#

CVE-Genie: An LLM-Based Multi-Agent Framework for Reproducing CVEs#

Saad Ullah (Boston University), Praneeth Balasubramanian, Wenbo Guo, Christopher Kruegel, Giovanni Vigna (UC Santa Barbara), Amanda Burnett (Arizona State University), Hammond Pearce (UNSW), Gianluca Stringhini (Boston University)

  • Why: LLM agents that reproduce known CVEs is automation aimed squarely at vulnerability triage and verification workflows — the kind of agentic systems work that signals where security operations tooling is heading.
  • What: A multi-agent LLM framework for reproducing CVEs. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: agents that plan and execute environment setup, patch/bug localization, and trigger construction to reproduce a vulnerability from its advisory — the multi-agent structure presumably divides these roles.
  • Where applicable: Vulnerability management pipelines, patch triage, and regression testing. The UCSB/BU security-lab pedigree suggests real CVE benchmarks rather than toy cases — unverified.

DeepProve: Verifiable End-to-End Large Language Model Inference#

Nicolas Gailly, Ismael Hishon-Rezaizadeh, Nicholas Mainardi, Shravan Srinivasan, Zack Youell (Lagrange Labs), Tianyi Liu, Yupeng Zhang (UIUC), Dimitrios Papadopoulos, Christodoulos Pappas (HKUST), Charalampos Papamanthou (Yale University)

  • Why: If inference outputs can be proven correct cheaply, it changes serving and audit economics for LLM platforms — clients could verify they got the model they paid for. A genuine ZK-research team (HKUST/Yale/Lagrange) attacking the LLM-scale end-to-end problem is a signal worth watching.
  • What: Verifiable end-to-end LLM inference using zero-knowledge techniques. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: ZK proofs over the inference computation (the full model forward pass or a faithful abstraction) so a prover can attest the output corresponds to the claimed model and input, without revealing weights or the input.
  • Where applicable: LLM serving with integrity or audit requirements; the open question is whether the overhead is practical at serving time — check the paper's performance numbers before taking it further.

Opaque Shader: Dynamic Code Execution on GPUs via Explicit Graphics APIs#

Hao Li, Yihong Hang, Yuan Xiao, Zhice Yang (ShanghaiTech University)

  • Why: Not a networking paper, but it speaks to what "GPU-safe execution" means: if arbitrary computation can be expressed through graphics APIs, then GPU sandboxing and compute-API restrictions have a bigger bypass surface than assumed. Relevant to anyone reasoning about GPU isolation.
  • What: Dynamic code execution on GPUs achieved via explicit graphics APIs. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: using the graphics pipeline (explicit APIs like Vulkan) to implement general-purpose dynamic code execution — i.e., treating shader stages as a Turing-complete target that compute-API restrictions don't cover.
  • Where applicable: GPU platforms generally; relevant to GPU sandboxing, security policy, and any model that assumes graphics and compute workloads are separable for isolation purposes.

Verifying Consensus Protocols from LLM-assisted TLA$^+$: A Case Study of Byzantine Reliable Broadcast#

Shuhe Cao, Xin Wang, Chenxu Wang (Shandong University), Xiao Sui, Sisi Duan (Tsinghua University)

  • Why: The PM works with distributed systems; this is the LLM-meets-formal-methods frontier in miniature — LLMs drafting machine-checkable protocol specs. Whether or not the specific case study matters, the workflow preview is where protocol verification is heading.
  • What: A case study verifying consensus protocols from LLM-assisted TLA$^+$ specifications, using Byzantine reliable broadcast. (No public abstract — title, authors and session only.)
  • How: Inferred: an LLM assists in writing/refining TLA$^+$ specifications, which are then verified mechanically — the human/LLM/model-checker division of labor is the interesting design point, and the case study (BRB) tests it on a protocol with real Byzantine complexity.
  • Where applicable: Distributed protocol design and verification workflows; the LLM-assisted spec methodology would transfer to other TLA$^+$-specified systems.

Themes#

  • Security research has converged on the PM's exact stack: eBPF, programmable data planes, DPU offload, GPU interconnects, and BGP/IXP/RPKI interconnect integrity — a full CCS track map onto infrastructure topics rather than abstract "cyber" threats.
  • eBPF is graduating from observability to enforcement: stateful in-kernel security policies and real-time multi-hop microservice access control signal that in-kernel policy is becoming production-real, while LLM-driven exploration of the eBPF runtime shows the verification/hardening side catching up.
  • GPU fleets are the new shared infrastructure — and the new attack surface: interconnect side channels (NVBleed), vGPU preemption attacks, prompt-reachable memory errors, and code injection on GPUs collectively read like the early multi-tenant-ethernet security literature, transplanted to accelerator clusters.
  • Interconnect trust is being stress-tested at its weakest links: IXP route servers and RPKI publication-point isolation — not the core protocols — are where the routing-security papers are aimed; both change operational trust assumptions the PM's peering strategy relies on.
  • AI is simultaneously the attacker's target, the defender's tool, and a workload with new failure modes: reasoning models as DoS surface (ReasoningBomb), multi-tenant serving isolation (TWIST), LLM agents auditing MCP tools, reproducing CVEs, generating eBPF, and drafting TLA$^+$ — the agent-as-infrastructure-engineer pattern is unmistakable.
  • Measurement and first-principles evaluation as pushback: studies of residential IPv6 exposure, registry bots, and graph-NIDS effectiveness ground the program in evidence rather than hype — worth noting how many strong picks here are evaluations of over-marketed system classes.
