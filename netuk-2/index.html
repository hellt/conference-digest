NetUK2 (The Brewery)#

A small, single-track UK operator event. The program is dominated by interconnection, physical-layer resilience, and honest failure stories — a good match for a PM whose core work includes physical DC/WAN systems. Several sponsor slots (Kubus/Juniper, Arista EVPN overview) and welcome/announcement items were reviewed and skipped: they are either pitches in disguise or carry no technical abstract.

July 8, 2025 Program

Core#

Analyzing network reliability up to 800G - Impact of SNR thresholds on BER for Coherent (16QAM) and Non-Coherent (PAM4) high speed transceivers under environmental variations#

Abstract

Dr Gerhard Stein (Lead Research Engineer), co-author Thomas Weible (Chief Technology Officer)

  • Why: As datacenter and DC-interconnect links move to 400G/800G, the margin between "link is up" and "link is silently degrading" narrows. This is one of the few operator-conference talks that quantifies that margin rather than asserting it, and the coherent-vs-PAM4 comparison maps directly onto DC/metro/WAN optics decisions the PM makes today.
  • What: A measurement-driven analysis of how close to a low SNR threshold 100G/400G/800G transceivers can run while still holding a tolerable BER, and how temperature and cable length shift that threshold — used to estimate how long a link can reliably sustain a connection between two transceivers. Coherent (16QAM) and non-coherent (PAM4) parts are compared head to head (write-up based on the abstract and program page; slides are Flexoptix-branded and the data was gathered with a Flexbox, so the measurement apparatus is Flexoptix tooling).
  • How: SNR is swept toward the failure threshold while BER is tracked, with temperature and cable length as controlled environmental variables, and the resulting curves are used to predict link reliability duration rather than a binary pass/fail.
  • Where applicable: Broadly applicable to anyone specifying or commissioning 400G/800G optics for DC fabrics or DC-to-metro interconnect — the physics is vendor-neutral even if the measurement rig is not. Expect the practical numbers to depend on the specific transceiver families tested.

Slash your cloud network costs with eBPF network monitoring#

Abstract Slides Recording

Mario Macias, Nikola Grcevski (Grafana Labs)

  • Why: Cross-AZ data transfer is a large, recurring, and poorly-attributed line item for anyone running Kubernetes in public cloud. This is a production-tested, OSS-only approach to attributing that cost to pods — squarely in the PM's eBPF/Kubernetes observability space.
  • What: A walk-through of how Grafana Labs cut their own cloud network costs using eBPF-based packet-level traffic analysis: identifying which pods and workloads generate elevated cross-AZ traffic, with zero application code changes and low overhead (write-up based on the abstract and program page summary).
  • How: eBPF performs packet-level analysis in the kernel; the low-level flow data is enriched with Kubernetes pod and node metadata so cross-AZ traffic can be attributed per workload, regardless of CNI, using only open-source tooling. The talk shows how to rebuild the same monitoring stack yourself.
  • Where applicable: Any Kubernetes-on-public-cloud environment where cross-AZ egress/transfer billing applies. The cost-saving framing is multi-AZ-cloud specific, but the eBPF-plus-pod-metadata attribution technique transfers to on-prem K8s traffic visibility generally. Rooted in Grafana's own production experience but the method is broadly adoptable.

Some interesting stuff I found on IX LANs#

Abstract

Ben Cartwright-Cox (bgp.tools)

  • Why: Every IX fabric is a shared Ethernet broadcast domain, and what actually appears on it versus what the AUP forbids is a recurring L2 security and hygiene problem. This is empirical, at scale — 100 peering exchanges observed from one vantage point — and directly relevant to anyone running or consuming IX infrastructure.
  • What: A survey of "bad" broadcast/multicast traffic bgp.tools has observed across ~100 peering exchanges' LANs, an assessment of the real-world impact of that traffic, and concrete suggestions for how exchanges could police it (from abstract only; the program page notes the slides are an evolving version previously shown at EuroIX).
  • How: bgp.tools passively observes IX LANs as part of its normal data collection, so the findings are longitudinal, real traffic rather than a lab reproduction.
  • Where applicable: Broadly applicable — the failure modes of shared L2 domains (stray ARP/ND, unwanted multicast, leaking protocols) are not IX-specific; the same lessons apply to any large shared switching fabric. The mitigation proposals are aimed at IX operators, but members can use the findings to check their own edge hygiene.

Detecting Non-Spoofed Traffic at ISP Ingress Points#

Abstract Slides Recording

Petros Gigis (University College London)

  • Why: "Traffic from source X arriving at the wrong ingress" is a daily operator puzzle: is it a route leak, a BGP hijack, a commercial-agreement mismatch, or just spoofed background noise? This talk offers a genuinely clever, deployable test to tell them apart — a small research idea with unusually direct operational payoff.
  • What: Penny, a traffic checker that distinguishes non-spoofed traffic aggregates arriving at unexpected ingress points from spoofed ones, with a planned open-source release (openPenny). Beyond the core detection, the tool is positioned to surface routing misconfigurations, BGP-hijack indications, upstream/downstream packet loss, abruptly terminated TCP flows, and load-balancing behaviour (write-up based on the abstract and program page summary).
  • How: The mechanism is elegant: when new traffic appears at an unexpected router, drop a few TCP packets. Non-spoofed flows retransmit; spoofed ones don't. The research contribution is making that robust — minimising harm to legitimate flows, tolerating path changes and remote loss that look like drops, and resisting spoofers who adapt to evade the probe.
  • Where applicable: Any ISP or large operator with multiple ingress points and BGP-speaking neighbors; the technique is TCP-dependent and ingress-diversity-dependent, so it fits transit/peering-heavy networks best. Research-stage (UCL) but explicitly aimed at open-source operator adoption.

How the Internet routed around Cable Damage in the Baltic Sea#

Abstract Slides Recording

Alastair Strachan (RIPE NCC)

  • Why: Submarine cable cuts (several in the Baltic recently under suspicious circumstances) are the canonical physical-layer WAN failure. This talk uses actual measurement data to show what happens to latency, loss, and routing when a regional cable plant degrades — evidence rather than folklore about redundancy.
  • What: An analysis of the impact of outages affecting the BSC East-West and C-LION1 cables, using RIPE Atlas measurements to track latency shifts, packet loss, and rerouting before and after the incidents. The program page summary reports the headline finding: only minor latency increases were observed, with inter-domain and intra-domain rerouting maintaining connectivity with minimal disruption.
  • How: RIPE Atlas probe meshes provide continuous latency/loss time series across affected paths; changes around the incident window reveal which traffic moved, where it moved to, and how quickly.
  • Where applicable: The specific event is Baltic-regional, but the methodology (Atlas-based before/after analysis of physical failures) is reusable by any operator for their own resilience validation, and the conclusion — meshed redundancy absorbs single/few cable failures — transfers to DC/WAN interconnect design generally.

Sorry we messed up#

Abstract Recording

Stefan Funke (Inter.link)

  • Why: A route leak that leaked "everything to everyone" is exactly the class of BGP incident every operator dreads, and this one was triggered by a seemingly innocuous change (renaming RCF functions) interacting with an Arista behaviour. Postmortems like this are the highest-value content at an operator event: the lesson transfers to anyone using vendor scripting/RCF for config automation.
  • What: A postmortem of a global route leak at a transit provider: the motivation for the change, how configurations are rolled out at Inter.link, why renaming Arista RCF functions produced unexpected behaviour and a global leak, and how it was detected and fixed (from abstract and program page summary; the speaker notes it is deliberately high-level, aimed at a general NOG audience).
  • How: Narrative postmortem: intended change → rollout path → surprise behaviour → blast radius → fix. The interesting mechanism is the interaction between operator-managed RCF function names and vendor handling of them.
  • Where applicable: The specific bug is Arista EOS/RCF-specific, but the transferable lessons — "cosmetic" config changes are not cosmetic, function naming indirection can silently change policy semantics, and rollout safeguards need to cover refactors — apply to any network with scripted configuration generation. Inter.link's global transit footprint made the blast radius unusual; smaller networks leak less far but the same way.

Power Consumption Considerations in Metro Optical Networks#

Abstract Slides Recording

Steve Jones (Unicorn Farmer)

  • Why: Optical transport power draw is becoming a hard constraint in DC and metro builds — pluggable choices, coherent DSPs, and port counts all trade against watts per bit. This pairs naturally with the 800G transceiver talk and the AI-DC power talk in the same program.
  • What: A look at the growing power needs of a metro optical network and the options available to mitigate them. This abstract is unusually thin — the program text literally says "Draft Powerpoint presentation attached," with the page summary adding only that it covers mitigation options — so expect an experienced-operator perspective piece rather than a paper (from abstract only).
  • How: Not described in the program material; based on the speaker's background this is expected to be a practitioner's walkthrough of where the power goes in a metro optical plant and what levers exist.
  • Where applicable: Metro/regional optical operators and anyone building DC-to-metro interconnects. Org-agnostic in principle, but with so little published detail, judge the talk on delivery.

Adjacent#

High Level Perspectives on Artificial Intelligence Data Centers#

Abstract Slides Recording

Geoff Bennett (Nokia)

  • Why: AI training clusters are reshaping DC networking demand — and the binding constraint is increasingly electrical power, not optics or silicon. For a PM whose portfolio spans physical DC networking, understanding the power budget conversation around AI DCs is near-term essential context.
  • What: A tutorial on resource trends for AI datacenters, focusing on power demand: how hyperscaler AI compute growth strains grids and water supplies, how published hyperscaler power strategies match up against realistic sources, and an assessment of enhanced geothermal and nuclear options (from abstract only).
  • How: Synthesis of published trends and vendor/ hyperscaler power strategies rather than new measurements — a well-informed survey talk.
  • Where applicable: Context for anyone planning DC capacity, siting, or fabric strategy over the next planning cycle. Vendor-delivered (Nokia), and explicitly high-level, so treat it as a framing talk, not a technical deep dive.

BNG Blaster Hands-On Training#

Abstract

Alex Le Heux, Christian Giese

  • Why: Automated, reproducible control-plane testing of access/routing gear is something most operators hand-roll badly. BNG Blaster is open-source, CICD-friendly, and this is a hands-on session with a pre-configured DUT — the kind of adoptable tooling an operator-focused program should surface.
  • What: A practical workshop on BNG Blaster, an open-source testing tool, covering access protocols (PPPoE, L2TP) and routing (ISIS, BGP) against a provided device under test, with exercises attendees can customise or extend (from abstract only).
  • How: Each participant SSHes into a pre-configured test environment with a DUT and works through provided or self-built test scenarios; prior familiarity via previous DKNOG/DENOG talks is recommended.
  • Where applicable: Primarily for operators of subscriber/edge infrastructure (BNGs, access concentrators) and vendors testing such gear; the general pattern — open-source protocol-level test harness in CI — transfers to any environment doing conformance/regression testing of network gear.

Wildcard#

Carbon-Aware Routing: Metrics and Challenges#

Abstract Slides Recording

Sawsan El Zahr (University of Oxford)

  • Why: Not a core interest for this PM, but carbon-aware routing is an emerging subfield that could eventually change link costing and TE policy the way latency-based TE did. It also connects directly to two other sessions in this program (metro optical power, AI DC power), making it part of a real trend rather than an outlier.
  • What: Research on making fixed wired networks carbon-aware: which carbon-related metrics can serve as link costs in link-state routing, what realistic topologies/traffic/grid-carbon-intensity data show about their limits, and CATE, a heuristic carbon-aware traffic engineering algorithm combining grid carbon intensity with router dynamic power draw and port power-down. The honest headline: no silver bullet, but promising directions that require no new router hardware (from abstract only).
  • How: Carbon-intensity signals and per-router power models feed a link cost/TE heuristic that reroutes or powers down ports to reduce emissions without equipment changes.
  • Where applicable: ISP-scale fixed networks with geographically diverse paths and dynamic power features in their routers; benefits are largest where grid carbon intensity varies meaningfully across a footprint. Research-stage (Oxford), evaluated on realistic but synthetic topologies rather than a named production network.

How to wash your servers (handling extremes of weather as a WISP)#

Abstract Slides Recording

Oli Stockman (Fram Broadband Ltd/NGA Connect Ltd/Superhero Broadband)

  • Why: Outside the PM's core areas, but this is a pure physical-infrastructure survival story: running a network when your office and primary POP are under four feet of floodwater. The lessons about physical vulnerability of head-end sites generalize to any DC or POP operator thinking about site risk.
  • What: A long-form talk on operating an outdoor-heavy WISP through wind, snow, heat and cold, centred on the October 2023 Storm Babet floods — how the network was kept running with the primary POP flooded, and the lessons learned (from abstract only).
  • How: First-person operational narrative — what failed, what was improvised, and what was changed afterwards.
  • Where applicable: Very org- and climate-specific (UK WISP, single-site POP, river flooding), so the specifics do not transfer directly. The transferable part is the thought exercise: what does your critical-site failure plan look like when the site itself is the outage?

Themes#

  • The physical layer is back as the failure domain. Submarine cable cuts, flooded POPs, and SNR margins on 800G optics — three separate sessions converge on physical infrastructure as the thing that actually breaks, and on how much design margin (redundant paths, headroom) operators really have.
  • Energy is becoming a first-class network design input. AI DC power budgets, metro optical power draw, and carbon-aware routing form a coherent thread: watts (and their carbon intensity) are joining latency and cost as optimization objectives.
  • eBPF has moved from novelty to cost engineering. The Grafana talk is representative: eBPF plus Kubernetes metadata used not for security or debugging glamour, but to put numbers on a cloud bill line item.
  • Measurement-driven debugging and honest postmortems dominate. Penny's drop-and-watch-retransmissions test, RIPE Atlas cable-cut analysis, bgp.tools' IX-LAN observations, and Inter.link's route-leak confession all share a pattern: instrument reality, report what actually happened, publish it.
  • The UK NOG scene is healthy but small-scale. Most talks are UK/regional operators or UK-based research (UCL, Oxford); hyperscale techniques appear mainly via vendors (Nokia, Arista), so treat the practitioner content here as bottom-up rather than hyperscaler-derived.
